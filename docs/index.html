You also get to pick what it hangs by — a fine thread, a round leather cord, twisted rope, or wooden beads — along with how big it is, how long the cord runs, and how much of a breeze it gets. None of it matters. All of it is adjustable.

The daruma keeps score

Tap it once to make a wish and it paints its left eye. Tap it again when the wish lands and it paints the right.

It remembers between reboots. So there is now a small wooden doll on your screen, patiently waiting to find out whether you meant it.

It knows when to leave

Start a screen share, a recording or a remote session and it takes itself down — charm, cord and hook — then hangs itself back up the moment you stop. Delightful on your own desktop; less so behind your head in a stand-up.

The rest of the time it hangs over your windows without taking your clicks. You can work straight through it — the only thing on screen that notices your mouse is the charm itself.

Getting one

One line. It fetches the latest release, installs it and switches it on.

curl -fsSL https://raw.githubusercontent.com/rahulpatwa1303/SudoCharm/master/get.sh | bash

Then log out and back in. GNOME will not pick up a new extension any other way. It catches everyone once, including me.

Would rather not pipe a script into your shell? Fair. Read it first, or grab the zip from releases and run gnome-extensions install --force on it yourself — there is nothing to unzip.

Where this came from

I saw Lucky Dangle, a lovely little Mac app that hangs a charm from your menu bar, and wanted one on Linux. This is my own build for GNOME — my code, my artwork, their idea. If you have a Mac, go and buy theirs.

Two questions people keep asking:

Is it really a pendulum, or just an animation?

Really a pendulum. The swing, the stretch in the cord and the charm's own lag behind the cord are worked out fresh every frame, which is why a flick makes it whip and why pulling it further down makes it swing slower.

θ''       = −(g/L)·sin θ − damping·θ' + breeze(t)
stretch'' = −k·stretch − c·stretch' + ω²·L·0.06
lag''     = −k₂·(lag − θ) − c₂·lag'

The idle sway is three slow waves of 6.7, 10.9 and 17.3 seconds. Those never line up, so it never repeats itself and never starts to look like a GIF. The charm on this page runs the same numbers as the one on your desktop.

Can I make my test suite ask for luck?

You can. It listens on the session bus, so anything that can run a command can give it a nudge.

# .git/hooks/post-commitsudocharm bless "$(git rev-parse --short HEAD)"# or make it earn its keeppytest && sudocharm bless || sudocharm flick 9
